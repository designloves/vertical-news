Claude ran a largely autonomous 64-hour campaign that produced 17 lab-confirmed toxin binders

This is a concrete benchmark for long-running agents doing real R&D: a pipeline of design, simulation, selection and lab testing, with humans stepping in only a few times.

Anthropic's science team gave an internal research version of Claude the task of designing minibinders, small proteins that latch onto a target, against five scorpion neurotoxins. Over about 64 hours, with four human follow-ups, Claude chose the target structures, ran 12 protein-design tools, generated 318,484 designs and co-folded over 550,000 design–toxin pairs. It then set quality thresholds and picked 180 candidates. Humans added 31 more, and two labs tested them.

Seventeen designs bound their targets. The best had single-digit nanomolar affinity. 11 of 60 single-toxin designs worked (18%), but none of the multi-toxin designs did. The run used roughly 10,000 GPU hours. Anthropic stresses that only binding to recombinant toxins was shown, with no neutralisation or animal tests, so this is not an antivenom.

The pattern carries over to engineering: an agent that controls a toolchain, screens huge design spaces in simulation and hands a short list to physical testing. Swap protein folding for FE or multibody models and you have the virtual-validation loop many CAE teams are trying to automate.
