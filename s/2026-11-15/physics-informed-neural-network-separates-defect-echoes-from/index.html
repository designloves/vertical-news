Physics-informed neural network separates defect echoes from ultrasonic guided waves and cuts scan points by seven times

Physics-based models of the healthy part could make ultrasonic inspection faster and easier to trust than purely data-driven defect detectors.

Researchers at the German Aerospace Center's Institute of Maintenance, Repair and Overhaul and RWTH Aachen University have posted a preprint, now under peer review, on finding damage with ultrasonic guided waves. They train a neural network with sine-based activations to obey the wave equations of a thin plate, so it acts as a model of the undamaged part. Subtracting that model from the measured wavefield leaves the waves scattered by a defect. A coarse scan then finds the defect and an adaptive scan refines it.

In tests on 3 mm aluminium plates, the full method reconstructed the wavefield with about 79% lower error than a data-only network and located simulated defects with a median error of 0.45 mm. On real measurements it found a milled notch within 2.63 mm. The adaptive scan needed about one seventh of the points of a dense scan, and inference takes under a millisecond.

The work covers flat plates only, but the idea of subtracting a physics model of the healthy part could carry over to inspecting rings, shafts and gears, where scan time and explainability matter.
