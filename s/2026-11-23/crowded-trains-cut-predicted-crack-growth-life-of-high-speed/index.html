Crowded trains cut predicted crack-growth life of high-speed wheelsets by up to 72%, mainly through load variability

Inspection intervals and load spectra for axles, shafts and bearing seats should follow load variability and overload cases, not mean load.

Researchers at Shijiazhuang Tiedao University in China and the Institute of Railway Research at the University of Huddersfield in the UK published a peer-reviewed study in Lubricants on how passenger load affects fatigue in a high-speed railway wheelset. They combined a vehicle-dynamics model with a finite-element wheelset model that includes the press fit between wheel and axle, counted load cycles with rainflow counting, summed damage with Miner's rule, and modelled a semi-elliptical surface crack.

Compared with an empty train, maximum fatigue damage was about 9, 20 and 102 times higher for seated, fully laden and crush-laden cases, and predicted crack-growth life fell by up to 72 percent. Under crush load, the mean vertical force rose only 19 percent, but the spread of suspension forces was almost three times larger. The groove next to the wheel seat was the critical location in every case.

The lesson carries over to driveshafts and bearing seats: overload and variability, not average load, drive damage, and press-fit transitions are where ultrasonic inspection should focus.
