Bristol researchers turn troublesome echoes into a non-contact ultrasonic array for finding defects

A non-contact receiver that uses geometry echoes instead of fighting them could help inspect curved drivetrain parts such as rings, shafts and gear roots.

In conventional ultrasonic array testing, sound bouncing between a defect and a part's surfaces creates noise and false indications. A team from the University of Bristol's School of Electrical, Electronic and Mechanical Engineering uses that scattering instead. A piezoelectric probe on an angled wedge sends sound in, and a laser interferometer records the surface response at chosen points without touching the part. Those points act as a virtual phased array, and the recordings are converted into a standard full-matrix dataset and imaged in the usual way.

Tested on an aluminium block with side-drilled holes, the wedge angle controlled which directions were covered, and a large-angle wedge improved image signal-to-noise by more than 5 dB. The paper is peer-reviewed and was published online on 12 November.

This is a lab demonstration on aluminium and drilled holes, not yet on steel bearing parts or real cracks. But a flexible, non-contact array could ease inspection of curved or hard-to-couple components where standard probes struggle.
