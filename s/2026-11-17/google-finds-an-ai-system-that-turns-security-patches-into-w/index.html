Google finds an AI system that turns security patches into working exploits with no human involved

The gap between a public patch and an automated attack is shrinking to hours, so web, backend and connected-vehicle teams need faster patching.

Google Threat Intelligence Group reports the first fully autonomous exploit pipeline it has seen in the wild. A framework called exploit_agent, found on an exposed staging server, was wired to several frontier and local language models. It compared popular web-application code before and after security updates, worked out what had been fixed, then wrote and ran exploits automatically. Telemetry pointed at French media, entertainment and education sites. Two months earlier, Google had seen no such pipelines. The report also describes a North Korea-linked group that altered AI coding-tool configuration files in victims' repositories to load a backdoor.

On the same day, the FBI warned about Artex, an open-source autonomous hacking tool. It says attacks went from targeting to compromise in under three hours, and that Microsoft 365 environments of at least 2,250 ConnectWise customers were accessed.

For frontend and platform teams, the practical takeaway is that patching speed now matters more than ever, and developer-tool configuration files have become part of the attack surface.
